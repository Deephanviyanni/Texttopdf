How to Write and Format Formal Letters

Written by: Professional Document Design Team • Published: July 2026

Formal letters remain the cornerstone of professional and legal communication. Whether you are applying for a job, submitting a business proposal, resigning from a position, or writing an official inquiry, the layout and formatting of your document play a critical role in how your message is received. A poorly formatted letter signals a lack of professionalism, whereas a structured, clean presentation ensures readability and authority.

In this guide, we will break down the essential components of a formal letter, explore different layout formats (such as Full Block and Modified Block), and review design best practices like margins, line spacing, and typography choices.

The Standard Structure of a Formal Letter

A formal letter follows a highly structured layout. Every piece of information has a designated location. Below are the key components in order:

  1. Sender's Address (Header): Place your contact details at the very top. Include your full name, street address, city, state, postal code, phone number, and email. In modern correspondence, personal headers are often centered or aligned right, while business letters are written on official letterhead.
  2. Date: Place the date on which the letter is finished directly beneath the sender's contact details, separated by a blank line. Write out the date fully (e.g., "July 24, 2026" instead of "07/24/26").
  3. Recipient's Address (Inside Address): Add a blank line after the date, and then list the recipient's information. Include their full name, job title, department, company name, and full address. Addressing your letter to a specific individual rather than a general department significantly increases response rates.
  4. Subject Line (Optional but Recommended): A brief, one-line summary of the letter's purpose (e.g., "Re: Application for Senior Marketing Position"). This helps the recipient quickly understand the topic.
  5. Salutation: Use a formal greeting. "Dear [Last Name]," followed by a colon is the standard. If you do not know the recipient's name, "Dear Hiring Manager," or "Dear Support Team," is preferred over outdated greetings like "To Whom It May Concern".
  6. Body Paragraphs: The core of your letter. Keep it concise, structured, and focused:
    • Opening Paragraph: State the purpose of the letter immediately.
    • Middle Paragraphs: Provide details, background context, arguments, or data supporting your purpose.
    • Closing Paragraph: State the expected next steps or call to action, and thank the reader for their time.
  7. Complimentary Close: Keep it professional. Use closing phrases like "Sincerely," "Respectfully," or "Best Regards," followed by a comma.
  8. Signature Block: Leave four blank lines for your physical signature, and then print your full name. Underneath your name, you can list your job title or organization name.

Formal Letter Layout Styles

There are three primary styles of layout used in formal business letters:

1. Full Block Format (Most Common)

In Full Block format, every element—from the sender's address to the signature block—is aligned to the left margin. Paragraphs are not indented; instead, you separate paragraphs using single blank lines. This layout is favored for its modern, clean appearance and ease of reading. It is the default formatting used in most official communications.

2. Modified Block Format

In Modified Block format, most elements are left-aligned, but the **Sender's Address**, **Date**, **Complimentary Close**, and **Signature Block** begin at the horizontal center point of the page. Paragraphs are still separated by blank lines and are not indented. This style offers a balanced visual layout and is widely used in traditional business sectors.

3. Semi-Block Format

Similar to the Modified Block format, the sender's address, date, close, and signature begin at the center. However, the first line of each paragraph is **indented** by 0.5 inches (or 5 spaces). This is the most traditional layout style and is rarely used in contemporary email attachments, but it remains common in personal, official correspondence.

"Regardless of the layout style you choose, consistency is key. Never mix block paragraphs with indented paragraphs in the same document."

Formatting Best Practices (Page Setup)

To ensure your formal letter prints perfectly and looks professional on all devices, follow these formatting dimensions:

  • Page Margins: Set margins to 1 inch (72 points) on all sides. For shorter letters, you can increase the top and bottom margins to 1.5 inches to center the text block vertically on the page.
  • Line Spacing: Use single spacing (1.0) inside paragraphs. Add double spacing (a blank line) between paragraphs, block sections, and around headers.
  • Typography Choices: Use a highly readable, standard font. Serif fonts like **Times New Roman** or **Georgia** convey tradition and authority. Sans-serif fonts like **Helvetica** or **Arial** offer a clean, modern look. Keep the font size between 10pt and 12pt for formal reading.

Final Checklist Before Downloading

Before downloading your formatted letter as a PDF:

  1. Proofread for spelling and grammar errors. Minor typos severely damage credibility.
  2. Ensure all placeholder data (like brackets, dates, or sample names) is replaced with your actual information.
  3. Verify page breaks. If your letter runs onto a second page, ensure at least three lines of text (the "widow and orphan" rule) carry over to prevent a lone signature block from sitting on a page by itself.
